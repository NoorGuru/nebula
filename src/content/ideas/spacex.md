## The problem

For half a century every orbital rocket was single-use: you built a skyscraper of precision hardware, burned it once, and dropped the pieces in the ocean. That economics kept launch prices in the tens of millions per flight and reserved orbit for governments and telecom giants.

## How it works

Founded in 2002 by Elon Musk and headquartered at Starbase, Texas, SpaceX attacked the cost problem with reusability. Its Falcon 9 lands its first stage propulsively and reflys it — a booster stage was first successfully reused in 2017, the same year a Dragon capsule reflown to the ISS — while the company flies at unmatched cadence (37 Falcon launches in Q2 2026 alone, 27 of them carrying its own Starlink payloads). Above that sits Starlink, the largest satellite constellation ever flown, and the Super Heavy–Starship system, designed for full reusability at heavy-lift scale.

## Pain points

Satellite operators faced punishing per-kilo prices and long waits for a ride; NASA needed cheaper crew and cargo access to the ISS; and billions of people had no affordable broadband — the demands that Falcon's flight rate, Dragon's crew runs, and Starlink's 12-million-subscriber network each answer.

## Business model

Paid launches first — commercial satellite missions, NASA crew and cargo contracts, national-security payloads — then recurring subscription revenue from Starlink broadband (ARPU of $66 a month in Q2 2026). Starship is the next revenue bet: commercial heavy lift plus NASA's lunar ambitions.

## Challenges

Reusability at Starship scale is unproven: the first orbital flight in September 2026 lost an engine on ascent, flew two orbits instead of six, and ended its splashdown in an explosion. Starlink's ARPU is sliding as it expands into cheaper markets, and the company's Moon-and-Mars promises face regulators, competitors, and physics all at once.

## Latest — September 2026

Starship's Flight 14 became the first to reach Earth orbit — deploying 26 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites — after an engine failure on ascent forced a shortened two-orbit mission that ended with an explosive splashdown; SpaceX still counts it as the program's breakthrough step toward operational heavy lift.

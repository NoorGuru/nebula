## The problem

Fusion promises clean, limitless power, but classic tokamaks are stadium-scale machines whose magnets cannot reach the field strengths a compact economical reactor needs — so commercial fusion stays permanently "thirty years away" while grids still burn gas.

## How it works

Founded in 2009 by Culham-lab veterans Alan Sykes, David Kingham, and Mikhail Gryaznevich, Tokamak Energy bets on the spherical tokamak: a compact, high-field design. Its ST40 machine became the first privately built spherical tokamak to pass 100 million degrees Celsius plasma ion temperature (March 2022, published in Nuclear Fusion). In parallel it built Demo4, a full tokamak-configured HTS magnet system that hit 11.8 Tesla — reproducing power-plant magnetic conditions — and packaged the capability as TE Magnetics, a division selling superconducting magnet systems for energy, research, medicine, and propulsion.

## Pain points

Fusion programmes need plant-relevant magnets years before any power plant exists; industrial buyers of high-field magnets face a thin, expensive supplier base; and the UK needs domestic manufacturing depth to deliver its STEP prototype plant at West Burton.

## Business model

Near-term magnet systems, contracts, and government programme work (STEP, US DOE milestone programme) fund the long-term prize: a fusion pilot plant design the company detailed publicly in 2024.

## Challenges

Net energy gain is still ahead of everyone; Commonwealth Fusion Systems and other US rivals have raised far more; magnet revenues must scale to carry a ~280-person team through a decade-long development; and delivery depends on public programmes staying funded.

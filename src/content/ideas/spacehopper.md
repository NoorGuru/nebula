## The problem

Asteroids are scientifically priceless and operationally hostile: gravity so weak that a wheeled rover risks bouncing itself off into space. Conventional rovers built for Mars or the Moon simply don't work there, so small-body surfaces stay largely unexplored.

## How it works

SpaceHopper embraces the microgravity instead of fighting it. The three-legged, nine-motor robot crouches and springs off the surface in a ballistic hop, then waves its legs mid-flight to steer its attitude — cat-like — so it lands feet-first on target. Short hops give precise local moves; big jumps traverse long distances; the legs double as landing gear, payload mounts, and a self-righting system.

## Pain points

No cheap scouting platform existed for rubble-pile asteroids; every hopping explorer was a PowerPoint until tested; and agencies want low-mass concepts that can piggyback on larger missions rather than flagship landers.

## Business model

None — it is a university research concept. Ten ETH Zurich students built it as a two-semester focus project under the Robotic Systems Lab; any future value flows through agency science missions, with the successor LunarLeaper already aimed at lunar exploration.

## Challenges

Graduating from parabolic-flight tests to an actual flight opportunity; hardening student hardware for radiation, vacuum, and dust; proving pinpoint landing on tumbling rubble; and funding a program that has no revenue model beyond research grants and agency partnerships.

## Latest — April 2024

SpaceHopper flew some 30+ microgravity parabolas aboard Novespace's Airbus A310 with the European Space Agency — lifting off under its own leg power in zero gravity and reorienting mid-flight, its first real off-Earth-environment test.

## Latest — December 2024

ETH Zurich confirmed LunarLeaper, the direct successor to SpaceHopper, tailor-made for exploring the Moon — the hopping concept's path from asteroid-lab prototype toward a lunar mission.

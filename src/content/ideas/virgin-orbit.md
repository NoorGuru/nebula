## The problem

The smallsat boom created demand for launches that were fast, flexible, and geographically convenient — but every orbital rocket flew from a handful of fixed government ranges with fixed schedules, leaving small payloads waiting in line or riding as second-class cargo.

## How it works

Founded by Richard Branson in 2017 and based in Long Beach, California, Virgin Orbit built LauncherOne, a two-stage rocket designed to be dropped from Cosmic Girl, a converted Boeing 747-400 operating out of Mojave. The airplane was effectively a reusable, relocatable first stage: take off from any long runway, fly to the drop zone, release the rocket to ignite mid-air and finish the climb to orbit.

## Pain points

Congested launch manifests, no responsive-launch option for urgent payloads, and no path for countries like the UK — with no orbital launch site of their own — to fly missions from home soil, which the January 2023 Cornwall attempt was meant to solve.

## Business model

Selling dedicated small-launcher missions: one rocket, one primary customer, on a schedule and flight path air-launch could tailor — pitched to commercial constellation operators as well as US and UK government payloads.

## Challenges

Air-launch never got the flight rate to pay for itself, and the company flew with almost no margin for error. When the high-profile Cornwall launch failed in January 2023, fresh funding evaporated: 85% of staff were laid off in March, Chapter 11 followed in April, and the May auction sold the company for parts — $16M of Long Beach facilities to Rocket Lab, Cosmic Girl to Stratolaunch for $17M, the Mojave site to Launcher (Vast) — for $36.4M total, with no buyer willing to operate LauncherOne intact.

## Latest — May 2023

Virgin Orbit announced on May 23, 2023 that it is closing for good after the bankruptcy auction raised $36.4M, with most of the ~100 remaining employees to be laid off within days and the LauncherOne rocket inventory left with no buyer.
